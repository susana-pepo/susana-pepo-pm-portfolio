How I work with AI, and what I have learned doing it.

Dub a video course at a fraction of the production time

Using AI dubbing to localize Trala's video lessons for new markets without a full studio cycle.

Localization In production

Trala's lessons are filmed once, in English. To open a new market I used AI dubbing to voice those same videos in the local language — turning what used to be a studio booking into a same-week task.

What I took away

  • The last 20% is still human. AI nails the bulk of the voiceover, but musical terms, names and timing against the on-screen hands need an editing pass before it ships.
  • Localization is a product call, not a translation task. I pick markets from retention data first, then dub — not the other way round.
  • Cheap enough to test. Cost per localized minute drops far enough that you can validate demand in a market before committing to a whole course.

From PRD to clickable prototype in an afternoon

Turning discovery notes into a working, shareable prototype with Claude and Google Stitch — no front-end help needed.

Discovery & Prototyping In use

Instead of writing a spec and waiting for a sprint to see it, I hand my discovery notes to Claude and Stitch and walk into the next meeting with something stakeholders can actually click.

What I took away

  • A prototype ends arguments a doc can't. People react to something tangible far faster than to a paragraph describing it.
  • Write the PRD like a brief to the model. Clear constraints in, usable output out — and the discipline makes the spec sharper for humans too.
  • Prototyping moved into discovery. I pressure-test the flow before a sprint is scoped, so engineering starts from something already validated.

A backlog that audits itself

A Jira-connected agent that flags stale stories and missing acceptance criteria, then posts a weekly digest.

Delivery In progress

Backlog debt stays invisible until it hurts a sprint. I'm building a Jira-connected agent that reviews ticket quality and turns that debt into a number the team actually sees each week.

What I took away

  • Make the invisible weekly. A recurring digest converts a vague "the backlog is messy" into a metric you can act on.
  • Defining "good" is the real work. The model is the easy part; writing down what a quality ticket means, precisely enough to check, is the hard one.
  • Automate the audit, keep the judgment. The agent flags and proposes — I decide what's noise and what ships.
